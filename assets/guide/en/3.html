The Training Pipeline: Pretraining Teaches Language, Post-training Teaches Instructions and Reasoning

Companion to diagram #3 · Chapter 1 "How LLMs Are Trained"

Diagram #3 draws the birth of a chat assistant as a pipeline: data → pretraining → base model → post-training (SFT, preference alignment, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards) → evaluation → deployment. It looks like a factory's process sheet, but each stage solves a completely different problem. Instead of explaining box by box, this article follows one question all the way through: how does a program that only "predicts the next token" become an assistant that answers, reasons and follows rules? What does it learn at each step, and what weaknesses remain?

1. The starting point: a probability table for the "next token"

Start with a fill-in-the-blank game. "Twinkle, twinkle, little ____": nearly everyone writes "star". "The weather today is very ____" has no single answer: nice, hot, cold, pleasant… but hardly anyone writes "expensive". List the possibilities by how confident you are and you have a probability table.

That is exactly what a language model does: given the preceding text, it outputs a probability distribution over the next token. A token is the smallest unit of text the model processes; it may be a character or several characters that often appear together (the next article covers this). Writing a whole passage just repeats the action: pick a token from the table, append it, and hand the new text back to the model to predict the next one.

So the training objective is very plain. Given real text x1​,…,xT​, make the model assign as high a probability as possible to the real next token at every position, that is, minimise the cross-entropy:

L(θ)=−t=1∑T​logpθ​(xt​∣x<t​)

You can read this through information theory. Shannon defined the information content of an outcome as −log2​p: the less likely it is, the more "surprising" it is when it happens, and the more information it carries. Cross-entropy is "how many bits per token, on average, it costs to encode the real text using the model's estimates". The better the model guesses, the fewer bits; if the model's probabilities matched the true probabilities of human writing exactly, cross-entropy would fall to the entropy of the text itself and could go no lower. In 1951 Shannon had subjects guess English text letter by letter and estimated its entropy at only about 0.6–1.3 bits per character; the longer the context they saw, the easier the next letter was to guess.

Why can such a plain objective produce "intelligence"? Because predicting the next token well takes far more than memorising word pairings. "The capital of France is ____" needs knowledge; "Tom is taller than Ann, Ann is taller than Ben, so the shortest is ____" needs reasoning; "He opened the fridge, found the milk had gone off, frowned and ____ it" needs common sense. Human writing carries knowledge, logic and intent, and to push cross-entropy down the model has no choice but to learn those regularities. This is a useful lens, not a theorem: that abilities emerge from this objective is an observed fact, repeated across experiments, but whether "predicting well equals understanding" is still debated (see article 7 of this chapter).

2. Pretraining: what do you get after reading the whole internet?

Pretraining runs the objective above over vast amounts of general text. How vast? Llama 3 405B used 15.6T text tokens and about 3.8×1025 FLOPs of pretraining compute, on up to 16,000 H100s at once. DeepSeek-V3 has 671B total parameters with only 37B activated per token, read 14.8T tokens, and its full training took 2.788 million H800 GPU-hours.

The GPT-3 paper showed this stage's most astonishing by-product. Trained only to predict the next token but scaled to 175 billion parameters, the model could perform translation, question answering and arithmetic without updating any parameters, just from a few examples in the prompt. This is called in-context learning.

Scale isn't picked at random. Kaplan et al. found that loss falls smoothly as a power law in parameters, data and compute; Chinchilla further showed that for fixed compute, parameters and training tokens should grow in equal proportion. So "how big a model, how much data" became an engineering question you can study with small models and extrapolate (article 4 of this chapter goes into detail).

But the product of pretraining has an obvious problem: it only continues text; it doesn't answer. Ask a base model "What is the capital of France?" and it may well continue "What is the capital of Germany? What is the capital of the UK?", because such text often appears in quiz lists online. It actually "knows" the answer: rewrite the question as "Q: What is the capital of Japan? A: Tokyo. Q: What is the capital of France? A:", a format usually followed by an answer, and it gets it right. The knowledge is already there; what's missing is the behaviour of responding as an assistant.

3. Post-training: three different "teachers"

Post-training uses orders of magnitude less data than pretraining, yet decides whether the model is pleasant to use. Think of it as three teachers who arrive in turn, each teaching differently.

The first teacher demonstrates: supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Collect demonstrations of "instruction + good answer" and keep training with the same next-token objective, computing the loss only on the answer. To the model a conversation is still one continuous text, with special tokens marking system, user and assistant. InstructGPT's SFT stage used only about 13,000 training prompts; LIMA went further, fine-tuning 65B LLaMA on just 1,000 curated demonstrations and getting strong conversational performance. Its authors proposed the "superficial alignment hypothesis": knowledge and capability come almost entirely from pretraining; SFT mainly teaches the format and style of answers.

The second teacher only compares: preference alignment (RLHF / DPO). High-quality demonstrations are expensive, and people are often better at comparing: you may not be able to write a good poem, but given two you can probably tell which is better. InstructGPT did it in three steps: labellers rank several answers to the same prompt → train a reward model on those comparisons → use PPO reinforcement learning to make the model produce high-scoring answers, with a KL penalty tying it to the SFT model. The result is telling: in its human evaluations, answers from the 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT were preferred to those from 175B GPT-3, a model over 100 times larger. For usefulness, post-training can matter as much as scale. DPO (2023) showed you can skip the reward model and online sampling and optimise directly on pairs of "better / worse" answers, which is far simpler to implement.

This teacher has blind spots. A reward model only approximates human preference, and Karpathy says RLHF is "just barely RL": unlike Go, there's no objective win or loss, and after long optimisation the model finds ways to fool the reward model, such as answers that grow longer and emptier. This is called reward hacking.

The third teacher only marks right or wrong: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). Maths problems have checkable final answers; code can run test cases. So the model can solve problems over and over, getting a reward when right and none when wrong, with no humans comparing and no reward model to game. DeepSeek-R1's report shows that training this way directly on the base model (R1-Zero) raised average pass@1 on AIME 2024 from 15.6% to 71.0% (arXiv first version; the version published in Nature in 2025 reports 77.9%). The model also spontaneously learned to write longer reasoning, check itself midway and go back to fix problems. Nobody taught it how to think; it was only told whether the result was right, and it discovered the strategy of "thinking a bit longer" on its own.

The three teachers' division of labour in one table:

Stage Data What it learns Main risks
Pretraining Trillions of tokens of general text Language, knowledge, latent abilities Only continues text; memorises some originals
SFT Thousands to hundreds of thousands of demonstrations Dialogue format, instruction following Can't inject much new knowledge
Preference alignment Human or AI comparisons Helpfulness, appropriateness, safety Reward hacking, alignment tax
RLVR Automatically gradable problems Reasoning accuracy Covers only verifiable tasks

4. The last two stops: evaluation and deployment

Evaluation answers "can this model ship?" The diagram lists capability benchmarks, safety and red-teaming, and blind human evaluation. They complement each other: benchmarks are cheap but can be contaminated by training data; red-teaming hunts for harmful output; blind human evaluation is closest to real experience but costs the most. InstructGPT also recorded that after RLHF, performance on some public NLP benchmarks dropped, the "alignment tax", which they mitigated by mixing pretraining gradients into PPO.

At deployment, inference has two phases: prefill (process the whole prompt at once) and decode (generate tokens one by one). The former is compute-bound, the latter memory-bandwidth-bound; KV cache management, batch scheduling and quantisation determine throughput and cost (article 6 of this chapter). An often underestimated fact: a model is trained once but called countless times, so cumulative inference cost often exceeds training cost, which is why architectural choices like MoE and GQA often serve inference.

5. What it means for architects

Seeing this pipeline clearly lets you judge "where should a capability come from?":

  • Knowledge and general ability are "bought", determined mainly by pretraining (data + scale). Most companies need not, and should not, pretrain from scratch.
  • Format, tone and domain phrasing are "tuned": prompt engineering or a little high-quality SFT is enough.
  • The latest or private facts are "looked up". Small-scale SFT mostly changes style; to inject many new facts, RAG or continued pretraining is more reliable.
  • Accuracy on verifiable tasks is "trained". If your task allows automatic grading rules (unit tests, validators), RLVR is currently the most effective route, which also explains why coding ability has improved especially fast.

Finally, three common misconceptions: fine-tuning does not reliably teach a model new knowledge; RLHF is not "real" reinforcement learning that improves without limit; and training is far from the end, since evaluation and inference are where the long-term costs lie.
