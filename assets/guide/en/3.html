The Training Pipeline: Pretraining Teaches Language, Post-training Teaches Instructions and Reasoning

Companion to diagram #3 · Chapter 1 "How LLMs Are Trained"

Overview

Nearly every general-purpose large language model (LLM) today follows the same pipeline: data preparation → tokenization → pretraining → post-training → inference. Pretraining is self-supervised next-token prediction over trillions of tokens of general text; this is where the model acquires language, knowledge and some reasoning ability. Post-training uses far less data — supervised fine-tuning (SFT), preference optimization (RLHF / DPO) and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) — to turn a base model that merely continues text into an assistant that follows instructions, reasons and behaves within bounds.

For architects this matters directly: it tells you which capabilities are "bought" (already in the base model), which can be "tuned in" through fine-tuning or prompting, and where the money actually goes.

Core Mechanisms

1. Pretraining objective. Given a token sequence x1​,…,xT​, minimize the autoregressive cross-entropy:

L(θ)=−t=1∑T​logpθ​(xt​∣x<t​)

The GPT-3 paper showed that with this objective alone, scaled to 175 billion parameters, a model can perform many tasks from few-shot prompts without any parameter updates.

2. Scaling laws. Kaplan et al. found that loss falls as a power law in parameters, data and compute; Chinchilla then showed that, for a fixed compute budget, parameters and training tokens should be scaled in equal proportion. This drives the first engineering decision: how big a model, and how much data (see article 4 of this chapter).

3. Post-training. InstructGPT established the classic three steps: SFT on human-written demonstrations → a reward model trained on human rankings of outputs → reinforcement learning with PPO. DPO later reduced preference optimization to a classification-style loss; DeepSeek-R1 showed that on automatically verifiable tasks such as math and code, pure reinforcement learning can elicit long-chain reasoning.

4. Inference. Serving has two phases — Prefill (process the whole prompt at once) and Decode (generate token by token). KV-cache management and batch scheduling determine throughput and cost (see article 6).

Key Papers and Milestones

  • 2017 Transformer: an architecture built entirely on attention; the backbone of every later LLM.
  • 2020 GPT-3: 175B parameters, in-context learning.
  • 2020 / 2022 Scaling laws: from "bigger is better" to "compute-optimal allocation".
  • 2022 InstructGPT: in its human evaluations, outputs of the 1.3B InstructGPT were preferred over the 175B GPT-3, despite 100× fewer parameters.
  • 2023 DPO: preference optimization without an explicit reward model or online sampling.
  • 2024 Llama 3: a public, flagship-scale training report — a 405B dense model on 15.6T tokens.
  • 2025 DeepSeek-R1: a reasoning model driven by reinforcement learning.

Engineering Practice and Numbers

All figures below come from the original reports:

  • Llama 3 405B: 15.6T text tokens, 3.8×1025 FLOPs of pretraining compute, up to 16K H100 GPUs.
  • DeepSeek-V3: 671B total parameters with 37B activated per token, 14.8T tokens, 2.788M H800 GPU hours for full training.
  • InstructGPT: post-training uses far less data and compute than pretraining yet greatly improves usability. LIMA likewise found that SFT on just 1,000 curated examples yields strong conversational behavior, leading its authors to hypothesize that almost all knowledge is learned during pretraining.

Rule of thumb: the capability ceiling is set mainly by pretraining (data + scale); post-training decides how those capabilities are used. For most enterprises the right move is an existing base or instruct model adapted via prompting, RAG or light fine-tuning — not pretraining from scratch.

Common Misconceptions

  • "Fine-tuning teaches the model new knowledge." A small amount of SFT mostly changes style and format; to inject many new facts, RAG or continued pretraining is usually more reliable.
  • "RLHF is real reinforcement learning." Karpathy argues RLHF is "just barely RL": the reward model only approximates human preference and gets gamed if optimized too hard. Verifiable rewards (RLVR) exist precisely to address this.
  • "Once trained, you're done." Cumulative inference cost often exceeds training cost, and architectural choices (MoE, GQA) are frequently made for inference.
