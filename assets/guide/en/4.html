Data and Tokenization: Text Becomes Tokens, Tokens Become Vectors

Companion to diagram #4 · Chapter 1 "How LLMs Are Trained"

A model never "sees" text directly. Before public class UserService reaches the Transformer, it goes through three steps: the data pipeline decides which text is allowed to be read at all; the tokenizer cuts text into a sequence of discrete tokens and turns them into integer ids; the embedding matrix then looks up each id and turns it into a vector. The left half of diagram #4 shows the first step, the right half the other two. They look like "preprocessing", but they directly set the ceiling on model ability, the efficiency of training compute, and whether users of different languages pay fairly.

1. The data pipeline: from web pages to a training corpus

Most pretraining text comes from web crawls, most commonly Common Crawl. Raw pages are full of navigation bars, ads, garbled text, duplicates and machine-generated junk; training on them directly wastes compute and teaches the model bad habits. So a typical pipeline is a chain of filters:

  1. Text extraction: pull the main text out of HTML, dropping templates and scripts.
  2. Language identification: route text by language so it can be mixed in proportion later.
  3. Quality filtering: first remove obvious junk with heuristic rules (too short, odd symbol ratios, too many repeated lines), then score text with a small model.
  4. Deduplication: remove large numbers of duplicate or near-duplicate pages with approximate methods such as MinHash.
  5. Safety filtering: remove personal information and harmful content.

How much is this pipeline worth? FineWeb offers rare systematic evidence. The team built a 15T-token dataset from 96 Common Crawl snapshots and ablated every deduplication and filtering choice: small models of the same size trained on each version of the data, and their downstream performance compared. More interesting is its subset FineWeb-Edu: a classifier picked out pages with "educational value", yielding 1.3T tokens that brought marked gains on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks such as MMLU and ARC. The conclusion is counter-intuitive: a smaller, filtered subset can clearly beat a larger unfiltered dataset.

Deduplication has another easily missed role: the credibility of evaluation. If benchmark questions leak into training data, scores inflate though the model hasn't really learned anything. And, as the next article explains, text repeated many times is more easily memorised verbatim, bringing privacy and copyright risk.

2. Tokenization: why not by character, and why not by word?

Suppose we feed the model characters. English has only a few dozen, so the vocabulary is tiny, but sentences become very long sequences: the model does a full computation for every token it generates, so longer sequences mean more compute and a shorter visible context. Splitting into whole words shortens sequences but explodes the vocabulary, and you always meet unseen words (new names, typos, variable names in code) that can only be marked "unknown".

Subword tokenization is the compromise: common words are a single token; rare words split into several common pieces. In 2015 Sennrich et al. brought byte-pair encoding (BPE) to neural machine translation. The algorithm is simple enough to do by hand:

  1. Start from single characters (or bytes); each is a symbol.
  2. Count every pair of adjacent symbols in the corpus.
  3. Merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol and add it to the vocabulary.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the vocabulary reaches the target size.

A worked example. Suppose low appears 5 times, lower 2, newest 6 and widest 3. In the first round, e s and s t both occur 6+3=9 times, tied for most frequent (say e s is merged first, giving es); in the next round es t also occurs 9 times and merges into est. (The end-of-word marker from the paper is omitted for the demo.) After a few rounds est is one token and newest splits as new + est. The merge rules are determined entirely by corpus frequency: pieces that often appear together are "welded" into one, so the model need not keep guessing obvious pairings, and the same context length holds more content.

BPE also has no "out-of-vocabulary" words: in the worst case any string falls back to single bytes. SentencePiece treats raw text, spaces included, as a sequence and needs no prior whitespace splitting, which suits languages such as Chinese and Japanese that don't separate words with spaces.

Karpathy, explaining tokenizers, stresses an engineering fact: the tokenizer is a completely separate stage of the LLM pipeline. It has its own training set and algorithm (BPE), and once trained it provides two functions, encode() and decode(); only then is the language model trained. So once chosen, the tokenizer is welded to the embedding matrix: changing it usually means retraining the model, or at least a lot of continued training.

3. The knock-on effects of tokenization

Tokenization granularity looks like a technical detail, but it reaches all the way to user experience and the bill.

Compression rate decides how much gets "read". Llama 3 expanded the vocabulary to 128K (100K from tiktoken plus 28K non-English tokens). Compared with Llama 2's tokenizer, the average characters per token on English samples rose from 3.17 to 3.94. The authors note this lets the model "read" more text for the same training compute. The extra 28K non-English tokens improved compression and downstream performance for those languages without affecting English tokenization.

Token count is cost. APIs bill by token, and context windows are measured in tokens. The same Chinese passage can produce very different token counts under different models' tokenizers. When estimating cost or whether a document fits in context, measure with that model's tokenizer instead of estimating from character count.

Vocabulary size is a trade-off. A larger vocabulary means shorter sequences, so attention and the KV cache are cheaper; but embedding and output layer parameters grow linearly with vocabulary size, and rare tokens get less training.

Some "silly mistakes" come from tokenization. Asking a model how many r's are in "strawberry", to reverse a long number or do exact multi-digit arithmetic often fails. One reason is that the model doesn't see individual characters but a few subword ids: it must infer the answer from indirect knowledge of which letters those pieces contain. The practical fix is simple: have the model write code to count, or call a calculator tool.

4. Embeddings: from id to vector

After tokenization, public class UserService becomes a sequence of integers, say [1212, 1888, 2938, 1860] (the numbers in the diagram are illustrative). Integers have no "meaning" of their own: 1888 and 1889 are no more alike than 1888 and 50000. The model needs a representation that can express similarity, which is the embedding.

With vocabulary size V and hidden size d, the embedding matrix is E∈RV×d, and the vector for token id i is row i of E. This step is just a lookup, but the numbers in the table are trained together with the rest of the model. Training pushes tokens that appear in similar contexts towards nearby positions, so the dot product between vectors can measure "how alike" they are.

Position must be encoded too. Attention by itself looks only at relevance and knows nothing of order: swap "dog" and "man" in "dog bites man" and, without position information, attention computes exactly the same result. The original Transformer added position vectors generated by sine and cosine functions to the token vectors; modern models mostly use rotary position embedding (RoPE). RoPE doesn't change the token vectors themselves; when computing attention it rotates the query and key by angles proportional to their positions. A dot product depends only on the vectors' lengths and the angle between them, and the extra angle from rotation depends only on the difference in positions, so attention scores depend on relative distance rather than absolute position. Llama 3 also raised RoPE's base frequency to 500,000 to support longer contexts.

5. A few judgements worth remembering

  • Data quality is worth more than data quantity. With fixed compute, spending on filtering and deduplication often beats simply adding data, as FineWeb-Edu shows.
  • The tokenizer decides how much the same money can read. For Chinese and other non-English use, measure token counts before discussing cost and context capacity.
  • For spelling, counting and exact-arithmetic errors, suspect tokenization before the model. Tool calls are usually more reliable than prompt tricks.
  • Tokenizers can't be swapped casually. One-to-one with the embedding matrix, a tokenizer is part of the model, not a pluggable preprocessing script.
