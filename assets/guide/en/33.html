Many Humans + Many Agents: People Define and Review, Digital Employees Execute

Companion to diagram #33 · Chapter 5 "Multi-Agent Programming"

Overview

When not one person but a whole team works with many agents, the problem shifts from tools to organization: who defines tasks? Who reviews? How are agent identities and permissions managed? The AIDC digital ad-buyer team in Alibaba's handbook went through three periods — "super individual → digital employees → cloud Scrum" — and is a real-world sample of this problem.

Core Mechanisms

1. The super-individual bottleneck. According to the handbook, after the team standardized on one mainstream agent tool, one person directing 3–5 sessions became normal, some long-running tasks completed overnight, and one person felt able to do the work of two or three, helping the team ship its first MVP. But problems soon appeared:

  • Digestion barrier: intermediate artifacts produced with heavy token spend were hard for humans to understand, review and apply; when discussing designs with colleagues, the colleague couldn't explain the design either and could only throw the question back to the agent.
  • Reuse barrier: after a task was delivered, the experience was hard for others to reuse.

2. Digital employees. The handbook treats agents as independent actors in enterprise systems: platforms used to answer only "does this person have permission"; now they must answer "which agent, in which runtime, on behalf of which user, based on which task, accessing which resource, doing what action, and whether human approval is needed". Human-agent collaboration goes through roughly three stages, essentially pushing the trust boundary outward:

Stage Trust boundary Human responsibility
Local assistance Human + local tools Provide context, approve commands, check results
Cloud autonomy Human + cloud runtime + repo Set goals, review MRs, key approvals
Multi-agent team Lead + specialist agents + many systems Design collaboration, govern boundaries, own final responsibility

The handbook cautions that the "intern" analogy only half works: agents need mentors, responsibilities, permission boundaries and review, but they have no professional ethics or implicit common sense, so governance also needs machine-enforceable guardrails.

3. Redrawing responsibilities. The digital ad-buyer team concluded: systems handle high-certainty matters such as state, permissions and rules; AI handles analysis, generation and long-running multi-step execution; humans handle goal setting, key decisions, risk trade-offs and final responsibility. The unit of delivery shifts from "people collaborating to complete tasks" to "people defining goals and acceptance criteria, digital employees executing their parts".

Key Papers and Milestones

  • Alibaba's AI Native R&D Paradigm Practice Handbook: digital-employee autonomy and trust boundaries
  • OWASP LLM01: human approval for high-risk actions, least privilege
  • MCP spec: explicit user consent before tool invocation

Engineering Practice and Numbers

  • Give agents their own identity: the handbook argues that reusing a person's account makes audits unable to tell human from agent actions; reusing a service account obscures who is responsible, who authorized it and who can disable it.
  • Review is a team-level bottleneck: the X developer's practice is keeping every MR small, with human review focused on business logic and whether test results are sufficient to support delivery.
  • Turn output into reusable assets: the handbook's team built two "cloud Scrum" supply lines: in the experience loop, two digital employees handle strategy discovery and launch respectively, cutting the strategy delivery cycle from about 10 days to 2; the hands-and-feet loop turns production failure traces into capability-building needs, with about 80% of core capabilities turned into Skills.
  • Limits of digital employees: the handbook is explicit that discovering growth opportunities still depends heavily on human business understanding; goal setting, business thresholds, release gates and high-risk decisions remain human responsibilities.

Common Misconceptions

  • "More agents means fewer people needed." The work of definition, review and governance grows with the number of agents.
  • "Running agents on a personal account is easiest." Audit and accountability become blurred.
  • "Digital employees can replace organizational consensus." The handbook notes they are better suited to executing, at high quality, work that has already been thought through and has clear standards.
