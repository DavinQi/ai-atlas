Post-training: From "Continues Text" to "Follows Instructions and Reasons"

Companion to diagram #7 · Chapter 1 "How LLMs Are Trained"

At the end of pretraining we have a base model that has read trillions of tokens and knows a great deal, yet isn't very "usable". Ask it "What is the capital of France?" and it may continue "What is the capital of Germany? What is the capital of the UK?"; it may write on without knowing when to stop, imitate rude online tones, or comply with harmful requests. The knowledge is there; what's missing is the behaviour of responding as an assistant.

Post-training supplies that lesson, using orders of magnitude less data than pretraining. Diagram #7 draws the mainstream route as four steps: 01 SFT, 02 RLHF, 03 DPO, 04 RLVR reasoning reinforcement. Each answers a different question: how to learn to answer? How to learn answers people like? How to learn it more cheaply? How to get more and more right on problems with a correct answer? We take them one at a time.

01 SFT: learn to answer from demonstrations

How. Collect demonstrations of "instruction + good answer" and keep training with exactly the same next-token cross-entropy as pretraining. Only two things differ: the data is human-written or curated dialogue, and the loss is computed only on the "assistant" part, because the model should learn how to answer, not how to ask.

A conversation is still one text. One turn is formatted with special tokens, roughly like this (each model's tokens differ):

<|system|>You are a helpful assistant.
<|user|>What is the capital of France?
<|assistant|>The capital of France is Paris.<|end|>

The "system prompt" is just the text at the very beginning; in multi-turn dialogue, all previous turns are concatenated as the context for the next; the model learns to emit the end token when it finishes answering, which is how it knows to stop.

How much data? Far less than you might think. InstructGPT's SFT stage used about 13,000 training prompts. LIMA went to an extreme: fine-tuning 65B LLaMA on only 1,000 curated demonstrations, its answers were judged equivalent to or better than GPT-4's in 43% of human comparisons. The authors proposed the "superficial alignment hypothesis": knowledge comes almost entirely from pretraining, and SFT mainly teaches the format and style of answers. A practical corollary: trying to pour lots of new facts into a model with a little SFT usually yields little.

Limits. Demonstrations are capped by whoever writes them. High-quality demonstrations are expensive, and on many tasks people are better at comparing than creating: you may not be able to write a good poem, but given two you can probably tell which is better.

02 RLHF: just say "which is better"

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) exploits exactly that. Reinforcement learning gives no reference answer, only a score, letting the model try things and adjust towards higher scores. Preference learning was first proposed by Christiano et al. in 2017 for deep RL, validated on text summarisation, and systematised by InstructGPT in three steps:

Step 1: SFT (as above).

Step 2: train a reward model (RM). About 33,000 prompts; labellers rank several outputs for the same prompt. Rankings are split into pairwise comparisons and trained with the Bradley–Terry model:

LRM​=−E(x,yw​,yl​)​[logσ(rϕ​(x,yw​)−rϕ​(x,yl​))]

It reads intuitively: yw​ is the preferred answer, yl​ the worse one, and the model should score the former higher; the larger the gap, the closer σ is to 1 and the smaller the loss. The reward model becomes a "stand-in for human judges" that can score countless new answers.

Step 3: PPO reinforcement learning. About 31,000 prompts. The model generates answers → the reward model scores them → high-scoring answers become more likely. But with one crucial constraint:

πθ​max​ Ex,y∼πθ​​[rϕ​(x,y)]−βDKL​[πθ​(y∣x)∥πref​(y∣x)]

The KL penalty ties the model to the SFT model πref​. Why tie it? Because the reward model only approximates human preference and has blind spots. Optimise freely and the model finds loopholes: if the reward model slightly favours long answers, the model writes longer and emptier ones. This is reward hacking. PPO itself, from Schulman et al., is a policy-gradient algorithm that clips the probability ratio between new and old policies to limit each update, likewise to avoid "going too far".

Results and costs. In InstructGPT's human evaluations, outputs from the 1.3B model were preferred to those of 175B GPT-3. For usefulness, post-training can matter as much as scale. But they also recorded an "alignment tax": after RLHF, performance on some public NLP benchmarks dropped, which they mitigated by mixing pretraining gradients into PPO (PPO-ptx).

Karpathy's often-quoted verdict on RLHF is that it is "just barely RL". Go has objective wins and losses, while RLHF's reward is only a gameable simulation of human judgement, and long optimisation exploits it. That is the problem the next two steps address.

Human labels can also be partly replaced by AI feedback: Constitutional AI uses a set of written principles to have the model produce its own critiques and preferences (RLAIF), reducing human labelling for harmlessness.

03 DPO: reduce reinforcement learning to a classification loss

RLHF is heavy engineering: train a reward model, sample online from the policy, and fit the policy, reference model, reward model and value network into memory at once. Rafailov et al. found that the KL-constrained objective above has a closed-form optimum in which the reward can be expressed by the policy's own probability ratio, so the reward model and RL loop can both be dropped and preference pairs optimised directly:

LDPO​=−E[logσ(βlogπref​(yw​∣x)πθ​(yw​∣x)​−βlogπref​(yl​∣x)πθ​(yl​∣x)​)]

Intuitively: raise the probability of the better answer relative to the reference model and lower that of the worse one, with β controlling how far the model may drift from the reference, the same role as the KL coefficient in RLHF. The paper reports DPO beating PPO-based RLHF at sentiment control and matching or exceeding it on summarisation and single-turn dialogue, without sampling from the model during training. Because it is simple and stable, Llama 3's post-training chose several rounds of SFT + rejection sampling + DPO, and the report says explicitly that it did not use more complex RL algorithms such as PPO.

DPO's cost is that it uses offline preference data: the model learns only from existing answer pairs and cannot explore new solutions itself.

04 RLVR: only mark right or wrong, and let the model explore

Maths problems have checkable final answers; code can run test cases. When answers can be checked by a program, reward no longer depends on a gameable reward model. Tulu 3 named this reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) and combined it with SFT and DPO into a fully open post-training recipe.

GRPO: drop the value network. PPO needs a value network (critic) as large as the policy to estimate "how much better than average is this answer?" GRPO, from DeepSeekMath, does it differently: sample a group of answers to the same problem and normalise by the group's mean and standard deviation of reward to get each answer's advantage directly. For example, sample 4 answers to one problem with rewards 1, 0, 1, 0 (right or wrong): mean 0.5, standard deviation 0.5, so the two right answers get advantage +1 and the wrong ones −1, pushing the model towards the former and away from the latter. No critic needed, and memory use drops sharply.

DeepSeek-R1: reasoning is "trained". R1-Zero ran RL directly on the DeepSeek-V3 base, without SFT, with entirely rule-based rewards: whether the answer is right (accuracy reward) plus whether the thinking is written in the required format (format reward). The paper states that it does not use neural reward models for reasoning tasks because they were observed to be prone to reward hacking in large-scale RL. During training, average pass@1 on AIME 2024 rose from 15.6% to 71.0% (arXiv first version; the Nature version reports 77.9%), and 86.7% with majority voting. More interesting is the behavioural change: answers grew longer, and the model spontaneously began re-checking, trying another approach, and going back to fix errors it found, which the paper calls an "aha moment".

R1-Zero also had clear problems: poor readability and mixed Chinese and English. So the released R1 used four stages: SFT on a few thousand cold-start long chain-of-thought examples → reasoning RL (adding a language-consistency reward) → rejection sampling to generate new data, combined with non-reasoning data for another round of SFT → a second RL stage for helpfulness and harmlessness. This also shows that the four steps don't replace each other; they are combined.

Choosing: which step fits your case?

Goal Recommended approach Why
Adjust format, tone, domain phrasing A little high-quality SFT (optionally LoRA) Superficial alignment; little data, quick results
Make answers match preferences better DPO Simple to implement, stable to train
Raise accuracy on gradable tasks RLVR / GRPO Rule rewards resist gaming; online exploration
Inject lots of new knowledge RAG or continued pretraining SFT mainly changes style, not knowledge

If you are building a post-training pipeline yourself, Tulu 3 released complete data, code and recipes based on Llama 3.1 (SFT → DPO → RLVR), a very good starting point.

Four common misconceptions

  • "RLHF can improve without limit." The reward model is an approximation, and the longer you optimise the more it is exploited, which is why the KL constraint exists and why reasoning tasks moved to rule-based rewards.
  • "SFT can inject lots of new knowledge." LIMA concluded the opposite: SFT mainly teaches format.
  • "DPO has fully replaced RL." DPO uses offline data; the big gains in reasoning came mainly from RL with online sampling.
  • "Alignment is free." InstructGPT recorded an alignment tax; post-training always trades off between objectives.
