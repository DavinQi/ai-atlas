The Transformer: Attention Lets Each Token Gather Context by Relevance

Companion to diagram #5 · Chapter 1 "How LLMs Are Trained"

Consider two sentences: "He was thirsty, so he drank a glass of ____" and "He was sleepy, so he drank a cup of ____". The first is most likely "water"; the second more likely "tea" or "coffee". Yet at the blank, the words that decide the answer, "thirsty" and "sleepy", are several tokens back. Or take "The trophy doesn't fit in the suitcase because it is too big": "it" is the trophy. Change "big" to "small" and "it" becomes the suitcase.

These examples show the core requirement of language modelling: every position must be able to gather information selectively from other positions in the preceding text, and what it gathers must change with context. The 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need answered with the Transformer: drop recurrence and convolution and model sequences entirely with attention. Today's LLMs are almost all its decoder-only variant. Diagram #5 shows one such layer: embeddings → masked multi-head self-attention → residual and normalisation → feed-forward network → residual and normalisation, stacked N times, finally outputting probabilities for the next token.

1. What attention does: a small retrieval

The crudest approach would average the vectors of all preceding positions. But then "thirsty" weighs the same as "was", "," and "so", and the useful signal is diluted. Attention turns the average into a weighted average, with weights set by relevance. It is like finding books in a library: each position computes three things from its own vector:

Name Intuition Library analogy
Query Q What information am I looking for? The topic you want to look up
Key K What information do I have? The label on a book's spine
Value V What do I provide if you attend to me? The book's content

Q=XWQ​, K=XWK​, V=XWV​, all three matrices learned. The computation has three steps: dot the current position's query with each visible position's key to get scores; turn the scores into weights summing to 1 with softmax; take the weighted average of the values. In matrix form it is one line:

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(dk​​QK⊤​+M)V

Work one by hand. The preceding text is "he / thirsty / drank / a-glass", and we gather information for "a-glass". Suppose vectors have only two dimensions: dimension 1 roughly means "related to drinking", dimension 2 "refers to a person" (a teaching construction; real model dimensions can't be read like this). The keys are (0,2), (2,0), (1.5,0), (0.5,0) and the values (0,1), (1,0), (0.8,0), (0.3,0). The query for "a-glass" is (1,0): "I'm looking for information about drinking."

  • Dot-product scores: he 0, thirsty 2, drank 1.5, a-glass 0.5.
  • Softmax weights: e0:e2:e1.5:e0.5≈1:7.39:4.48:1.65, normalised to about 6.9%, 50.9%, 30.9% and 11.4%.
  • Weighted average: dimension 1 ≈0.509×1+0.309×0.8+0.114×0.3≈0.79, dimension 2 ≈0.069.

What "a-glass" gathers comes mainly from "thirsty" and "drank"; the "drinking" dimension is strong, so predicting "water" becomes much easier. Nobody decreed "attend to thirsty": attending this way helps lower cross-entropy, so the corresponding WQ​ and WK​ gradually form during training.

Why divide by dk​​? The higher the dimension, the more terms a dot product sums and the larger it gets. When scores differ too much, softmax gives almost all weight to one position and gradients become tiny. Scaling keeps the distribution gentle.

The causal mask M. During generation the model knows only what has been written, and training must match: each position may see only itself and earlier positions. Future positions' scores are set to −∞, so their weights become 0 after softmax. This brings a key efficiency advantage: a 1,000-token text needs just one forward pass, with every position simultaneously predicting its own next token, effectively about 1,000 practice questions at once. That is the fundamental reason Transformers train so efficiently in parallel on GPUs.

2. Multiple heads: asking several questions at once

A sentence holds many relationships at once: who did what, word collocations, reference, topic. One set of Q/K/V can look things up in only one way. In the example, change the query for "a-glass" to (0,1), meaning "who did this?", and attention concentrates on "he". So the Transformer splits the d dimensions into h heads, computes attention in each, concatenates the results and mixes them with a matrix.

Researchers have found heads with clear functions in real models. One example is the induction head: if "A B" appeared earlier, then on meeting "A" again it attends to the "B" that followed last time and so predicts "B". Olsson et al. argue that such heads are an important mechanism by which models "learn on the fly" from the prompt. But most heads are nowhere near this tidy, which is one reason the claim that "attention weights are the model's explanation" doesn't hold up.

KV heads can be shared. During decoding every step reads the K/V of all past positions, and more heads mean more to read. Multi-query attention (MQA) has all query heads share one set of K/V, greatly speeding up decoding; grouped-query attention (GQA) compromises with several shared groups, approaching multi-head quality at near-MQA speed. Llama 3's 8B, 70B and 405B all use only 8 KV heads, shrinking the KV cache to nkv​/nheads​ of the multi-head size (article 6 works through the numbers).

3. The feed-forward network: back to your desk to digest

Attention handles "exchanging information between positions", like a meeting; afterwards each position passes through a feed-forward network (FFN) on its own, like going back to your desk to digest. The FFN widens the vector to about 4×, applies a non-linear function, then projects back. Attention is mostly weighted averaging; most of the complex non-linear transformation happens in the FFN.

The FFN also holds most of the parameters. With width d, a layer's attention has four d×d matrices (Q, K, V, output), about 4d2; the FFN has two matrices, d→4d and 4d→d, about 8d2, for about 12d2 in total. Plug in GPT-3's d=12288 and 96 layers: about 1.81 billion per layer, about 173.9 billion over 96 layers, plus an embedding table of about 0.62 billion, totalling about 174.6 billion, almost exactly the published 175 billion. The FFN accounts for about two thirds. Geva et al. found that FFNs behave like "key–value memories": some parameters detect patterns in the input, others raise the probability of certain following tokens accordingly. But knowledge is spread across vast numbers of parameters; you can't point to one and say "this is where 'Paris is the capital of France' is stored".

4. Making dozens of layers stack: residuals and normalisation

Every sublayer is wrapped in a residual connection: x←x+sublayer(Norm(x)). Think of it as a "residual stream" running through the whole model: each layer only writes increments onto it, so existing information is not overwritten and gradients flow straight back to the bottom layers. Placing normalisation before the sublayer (Pre-Norm) trains more stably than the original placement after the residual.

Compared with the 2017 original, modern LLMs have also generally swapped a few components:

  • RMSNorm: scales by the root mean square only, without subtracting the mean; cheaper with comparable results.
  • SwiGLU FFN: a gated linear unit variant that performs better at the same compute.
  • RoPE position encoding: makes attention scores depend only on relative position (see the previous article).

Llama 3 is a textbook modern dense Transformer: 8B has 32 layers and hidden size 4,096; 70B has 80 layers and 8,192; 405B has 126 layers and 16,384. All three use SwiGLU, RoPE (θ=500,000), a 128K vocabulary and 8 KV heads.

5. The quadratic cost, and FlashAttention

Attention scores every position against every visible position, so time and memory grow with the square of sequence length. Extending context from 4K to 128K is 32× longer, making the attention matrix roughly 1,000× larger.

FlashAttention doesn't change the maths; it changes memory access. GPUs compute fast, and the bottleneck is often moving data between high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and on-chip SRAM. It tiles Q, K and V into SRAM, finishes softmax and the weighted sum within each block, then writes back, never writing the huge attention matrix to HBM. The result is identical to standard attention; the paper reports 3× end-to-end training speed-up on GPT-2 (sequence length 1K), and FlashAttention-2 further improved parallelism and work partitioning. PyTorch's SDPA, vLLM and other frameworks integrate such kernels by default today; reuse them before writing your own.

6. Three common misconceptions

  • "Attention weights are the model's explanation." Attention reflects only part of the information routing; FFNs, the residual stream and the combination of layers all take part in the decision, so it cannot be read directly as a causal explanation.
  • "The model can use everything in a long context window." Window length is an architectural and training ceiling; whether the model can actually use distant information must be evaluated separately.
  • "FlashAttention is an approximation." It is exact attention; only the order of computation and memory access differ.
