Training Cost: Each Step Up in Scale Is a Jump in Order of Magnitude

Companion to diagram #10 · Chapter 1 "How LLMs Are Trained"

Diagram #10 is a cost table ordered by parameter count: 7B, 13B, 34B, 70B, 400B+. Each row gives pretraining tokens, pretraining cost, SFT cost and alignment cost, and a bar at the bottom shows the cost breakdown for a 70B model, with pretraining taking about 94%. The table is the speaker's order-of-magnitude estimate, meant to build intuition.

This article doesn't restate or replace those numbers. Instead it does two things: gives a method you can recompute yourself, so that faced with any "how much does it cost to train an X-billion-parameter model?" question you can estimate the order of magnitude in minutes; and uses real bills from public reports as anchors to show which costs are most easily missed.

1. A four-step estimate

Step 1: compute FLOPs. Training compute is about C≈6ND (N parameters, D training tokens; derived in article 4).

Step 2: convert to GPU-hours.

GPU-hours≈peak FLOPS per GPU×MFU×36006ND​

Two numbers here are the easiest to get wrong. The first is peak compute: the H100 SXM's official BF16 figure is 1,979 TFLOPS, but that is "with sparsity"; dense matrix multiplication is about half, roughly 989 TFLOPS. The second is MFU (model FLOPs utilisation): the share of peak that is actually useful. Communication, memory access and pipeline bubbles all eat compute. Llama 3 405B's BF16 MFU on 8K–16K H100s was 38–43%, and that is top-team level.

Step 3: multiply by the unit price. For your own cluster, count depreciation, power and operations; for rented capacity, the price per GPU-hour. Prices vary greatly by hardware, contract and region.

Step 4: add what gets overlooked. Failed reruns, experiments and ablations, post-training, data and people (section 3).

2. Work an example

Take 70B parameters and 2.2T tokens (matching the token count in the diagram's 70B row):

  • C=6×7×1010×2.2×1012≈9.2×1023 FLOPs
  • At 989 TFLOPS and 40% MFU, effective compute per GPU per second is about 3.96×1014 FLOPs
  • GPU-hours ≈9.2×1023/(3.96×1014×3600)≈650,000 H100-hours
  • At $2 per GPU-hour (the same H800 rental assumption DeepSeek-V3's report uses), about $1.3 million
  • On 2,048 GPUs, about 650,000/2048≈317 hours, about 13 days

This is only the raw compute of one production training run. The diagram's estimates include other costs and give ranges, on a different basis, so the two shouldn't be compared directly.

Which number is most sensitive? Break the formula apart: cost is proportional to both N and D. Double the parameters or double the data and cost doubles; let MFU fall from 40% to 20% and cost doubles too. So engineering efficiency (MFU) and modelling decisions (the N/D split) affect the bill on the same order. Moreover, following Chinchilla, D usually grows with N, so total cost scales roughly with N2: a model 10× larger may cost about 100× more to train. That is why costs in the diagram rise much faster than parameters from row to row.

MoE is counted by active parameters. DeepSeek-V3 has 671B total parameters with 37B active per token, and its report says pretraining needs only 180,000 H800-hours per trillion tokens. Plug in the active parameters: 6×3.7×1010×1012=2.2×1023 FLOPs, consistent with the report's order of magnitude. Mistakenly using the 671B total would overestimate by about 18×.

3. A real bill, and what's outside it

DeepSeek-V3 published its complete GPU-hour bill:

Stage H800 GPU-hours
Pretraining (14.8T tokens) 2,664K
Context extension 119K
Post-training 5K
Total 2,788K

At $2 per GPU-hour the total is $5.576 million. The figure caused wide discussion in early 2025. The report itself is clear: it covers only the production training run, not earlier research and ablations on architecture, algorithms and data. Karpathy commented at the time that this amounted to about 2 months on 2,048 GPUs for about $6 million, whereas comparable capability had been thought to need a cluster closer to 16K GPUs. What it shows is progress in engineering efficiency (FP8 training, MoE, communication optimisation), not that "a frontier model costs only $6 million".

Outside the bill there are at least four other kinds of cost:

Failures and effective training time. With tens of thousands of GPUs running at once, hardware failure is routine. Llama 3 405B hit 466 job interruptions in 54 days of training, 419 of them unexpected, about 78% attributed to confirmed or suspected hardware issues. With automated detection and fast recovery, the team still achieved more than 90% effective training time. Teams without that engineering capability may get much less, which magnifies cost directly.

R&D and people. Epoch AI's estimates for frontier models such as GPT-4 and Gemini show the two largest expenses are AI accelerator chips and staff, each tens of millions of dollars; server components are 15–22%, cluster interconnect 9–13%, and energy only 2–6%. Many people assume electricity is the big item; it is actually the smallest.

The growth trend. Since 2016, the amortised training cost of the most compute-intensive models has grown about 2.4× per year (90% confidence interval 2.0–2.9×). On this trend, the largest training runs will exceed $1 billion by 2027.

The share of post-training. The diagram's bar shows pretraining at about 94%. DeepSeek-V3's post-training was only about 0.2% of total GPU-hours (5K / 2,788K), consistent with that. But with the rise of reasoning models the share of RL training is growing, and models differ widely, so don't generalise.

4. What it means for companies

Pretraining from scratch is almost never optimal. For a 70B-class model, the compute of one production run alone is on the order of a million dollars; add failures, experiments and people and the real investment is several times higher, and it needs a team that can sustain 40% MFU and 90% effective time. Most scenarios should use an open-source or commercial base model and adapt it with prompt engineering, RAG or light fine-tuning.

Fine-tuning costs are a different order of magnitude. With the same formula, fine-tuning a 7B model on 100 million tokens takes 6×7×109×108≈4.2×1018 FLOPs, just a few H100-hours; parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA cut memory needs further.

Inference is the long-term bill. A model is trained once but serves countless requests. When estimating total cost of ownership, multiply expected call volume by per-call inference cost; that number often exceeds training cost within a year.

5. Three common misreadings

  • "DeepSeek-V3 built a frontier model for only $5.6 million." That is the production run converted at a rental price, excluding research, ablations, people and hardware purchases.
  • "Cost depends only on parameter count." At the same parameter count, doubling tokens doubles compute; halving MFU doubles cost.
  • "MoE training cost is counted by total parameters." Compute follows active parameters, but memory, communication and load balancing add extra engineering cost.
