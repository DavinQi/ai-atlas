Common Misconceptions: Looking Like It Isn't Being It

Companion to diagram #9 · Chapter 1 "How LLMs Are Trained"

Overview

The diagram lists three common misunderstandings: "the model understands and thinks like a human brain", "the model stores the original text of every book", and "the model keeps learning new knowledge after training". This article goes through what the evidence does and does not support. One caveat up front: the first point touches on the definition of "understanding", which is still debated; this article only states mechanism-level facts that can be tested experimentally and draws no philosophical conclusions.

Core Mechanisms

Misconception 1: "It understands and thinks like a human brain"

Mechanism. An LLM is trained to minimize next-token cross-entropy −∑t​logpθ​(xt​∣x<t​). At generation time it outputs a probability distribution over the next token, and a decoding strategy samples from it. "Thinking", in reasoning models, means generating a chain of thought before the answer. Chain-of-thought prompting substantially improves multi-step reasoning; DeepSeek-R1 used reinforcement learning to make longer chains of thought and self-checking behaviors emerge on their own.

How to read this. "It just predicts the next token" describes the training objective and output interface, not how complex the internal representations are: predicting the next token well requires learning a great deal of grammar, facts and reasoning patterns. At the same time, behavior is heavily shaped by training data. Karpathy suggests that instead of mystifying "asking an AI", think of it as "asking the average data labeler", since an assistant's answering style is largely imitated from human-labeled data.

Engineering takeaway. Don't treat model output as verified fact; for tasks that need correctness, check it in verifiable ways (tests, retrieved evidence, rule-based validation).

Misconception 2: "It stores the original text of every book"

Mechanism. Parameter count is far smaller than the training corpus: Llama 3 405B has 405 billion parameters and was trained on 15.6T tokens, so it cannot store the corpus verbatim. The model learns a compressed representation of statistical regularities, which is why it forgets details and "hallucinates" — producing plausible content that contradicts facts or the input.

But "stores no original text at all" is also wrong. Carlini et al. extracted hundreds of verbatim training sequences from GPT-2, including personal information such as phone numbers and emails — some of which appeared in only one document in the training data. Follow-up work found memorization grows significantly with model capacity, the number of times an example is duplicated, and the length of the prompt context.

A more accurate statement: the model is not a retrievable database of source text, but it does memorize some content verbatim, especially heavily duplicated text. This creates privacy and copyright risk and is one reason to deduplicate training data.

Misconception 3: "It keeps learning new knowledge after training"

Mechanism. Deployed weights are fixed. Information given in a conversation lives only in that session's context window (in-context learning); it disappears when the session ends and is never written back to the weights. Product "memory" features store history externally and put it back into the context when needed.

How to give a model new knowledge:

  • RAG (retrieval-augmented generation): retrieve external documents into the context at generation time. Lewis et al. note that combining parametric and non-parametric memory provides provenance and makes knowledge easier to update.
  • Continued training or fine-tuning: changes the weights, but is expensive, and a small amount of SFT mainly changes format rather than knowledge.

Karpathy's summary fits well: pretraining is for knowledge, fine-tuning (supervised or RL) is for habitual behavior, and both change parameters; he argues we are still missing a learning paradigm in which models learn on the job the way people do.

Engineering Practice and Numbers

  • For time-sensitive, traceable knowledge: prefer RAG and require citations in the output.
  • For fine-tuning on private data: mind memorization risk; deduplicate and filter sensitive information.
  • For outputs that drive key decisions: add a verification step; don't rely on the model's confident tone.
