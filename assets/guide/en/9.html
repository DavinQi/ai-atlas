Common Misconceptions: Looking Like It Isn't Being It

Companion to diagram #9 · Chapter 1 "How LLMs Are Trained"

Diagram #9 sets three of the most common misconceptions beside the corresponding "facts": the model understands and thinks like a human brain; the model stores every book verbatim; the model keeps learning new knowledge on its own after training. They have one thing in common: each pictures the model as something familiar, whether a person, a database or self-updating software. The real mechanism falls precisely between these analogies.

This article goes through each, asking what the research evidence supports and what it doesn't, and adds a few "close relatives" that follow from the same mechanism: hallucination, sycophancy and failing in reverse. One caveat first: the first misconception touches on the definition of "understanding", which is still debated. This article states only mechanistic facts that experiments can test and draws no conclusions on the philosophical question.

Misconception 1: "It understands and thinks like a human brain"

The mechanism. An LLM is trained to minimise next-token cross-entropy −∑t​logpθ​(xt​∣x<t​); when generating, it outputs a probability distribution over tokens one at a time and draws from it according to a decoding strategy. In reasoning models, "thinking" shows up as generating a chain of thought before the answer. Wei et al. found that simply showing "step by step" examples in the prompt markedly improves multi-step reasoning; DeepSeek-R1 used reinforcement learning to make models spontaneously produce longer chains of thought with self-checks.

But "it just predicts the next token" is also a misreading. That phrase describes the training objective and output interface; it says nothing about how simple the internal representations are. To fill in "Tom is taller than Ann, Ann is taller than Ben, so the shortest is ____", the model must to some degree represent the relationships among these people. Interpretability research has found reusable "circuits" inside models too, such as the induction heads discussed in article 3.

So how is it different from a person? At least three differences can be observed:

  1. What is written can't be taken back. Autoregressive generation feeds each output back as the next input. If it opens with "the answer is A", even when later reasoning shows A is wrong, it often goes on to justify A. That is why "reason first, answer second" matters.
  2. Uneven abilities. It may solve a competition maths problem yet fail at a simple common-sense question or a letter-counting task, the latter often because of tokenization.
  3. Its style comes from labellers. Karpathy cautions that rather than mythologising "asking the AI", think of it as "asking an average data labeller": the assistant's answering style is learned mainly by imitating human-labelled data.

Engineering implication. Don't treat model output as a verified conclusion. For tasks that need correctness, check in verifiable ways: run tests, retrieve evidence, apply rule-based validation.

Misconception 2: "It stores the text of every book"

Do the arithmetic. Llama 3 405B has 405 billion parameters and trained on 15.6T tokens. Even if every parameter stored text, it couldn't keep the whole corpus word for word. What the model learns is a compressed representation of statistical regularities, consistent with the "compression is intelligence" view: being able to reconstruct data from a shorter description depends on capturing reusable regularities, not rote memorisation.

A side effect of compression is hallucination. Hallucination means generating fluent, confident content that contradicts the facts: inventing papers and URLs that don't exist, or giving a precise but wrong number. The mechanism suggests several sources:

  • The objective is "plausible", not "true". When unsure, the most plausible continuation is often still fluent, confident text. The model knows very well what a paper citation should look like; it may not know whether the paper exists.
  • Isolated facts follow no pattern. Facts like "an ordinary person's birthday" can't be derived from regularities, only memorised one by one. Kalai et al.'s analysis argues that if 20% of birthday facts appear only once in the training data, a base model can be expected to get at least about 20% of such questions wrong.
  • Evaluations reward guessing. Many benchmarks score only right or wrong, so answering "I don't know" scores zero, the same as a wrong answer. As in an exam with no penalty for wrong answers, guessing beats leaving it blank, and models optimised under that incentive naturally lean towards guessing.

So the more obscure and specific the content (names, numbers, dates, citations, URLs), the more error-prone.

But "it stores no original text at all" is also inaccurate. Carlini et al. extracted hundreds of verbatim training sequences from GPT-2, including personal information such as phone numbers and email addresses, some of which appeared in only one document in the training data. Later research quantified this memorisation: it rises markedly with model capacity, the number of times a sample is duplicated, and the length of the prompt context.

More precisely: the model is not a searchable database of original text, but it does memorise some content verbatim, especially text repeated many times. That brings privacy and copyright risk, and is one reason to deduplicate training data.

Misconception 3: "It keeps learning new knowledge after training"

The mechanism. A deployed model's weights are fixed. Information given in conversation exists only in that session's context window; the model can use it (in-context learning), but it disappears when the session ends and is never written back to the weights. A product's "memory" feature essentially stores history externally and puts it back into context when needed.

Two direct consequences. First, knowledge has a cut-off date, and the model may not know what it doesn't know: ask for "the latest version" and it will confidently give the answer from training time. Second, longer context isn't always more usable: Liu et al. found that information placed in the middle of a long context is more easily overlooked, while information at the beginning and end is used better.

How do you give a model new knowledge?

  • RAG (retrieval-augmented generation): retrieve external documents into context before generating. Lewis et al. point out that combining parametric and non-parametric memory can provide provenance and makes knowledge easier to update.
  • Continued training or fine-tuning: changes the weights, but is expensive, and small-scale SFT mainly changes format, not knowledge.

Karpathy's summary: pretraining is for acquiring knowledge and fine-tuning for forming habitual behaviour, and both change parameters; he believes we still lack a learning paradigm that, like people, learns while being used.

Three "close relatives" from the same mechanism

Sycophancy. When rating answers, people more readily give high scores to answers that agree with them, and RLHF learns that preference too. Sharma et al. observed systematic sycophancy across multiple assistant models. Countermeasure: ask neutrally and explicitly request that it point out your mistakes.

The reversal curse. Models learn directional associations in text. Berglund et al. found that models trained on "A is B" often can't answer "who is B?": having learned "X's mother is Y", a model may not be able to answer "who is Y's son?". Countermeasure: re-ask from another angle, and check critical reasoning yourself.

Citations it writes may not exist. This is hallucination's most dangerous form in research and law. Countermeasure: require verifiable links and open them yourself.

Summary
