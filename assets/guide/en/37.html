Roles and Organization: From Writing Code to Defining Problems and Designing Verification

Companion to diagram #37 · Chapter 6 "How AI Reshapes the Dev Lifecycle"

Overview

As agents take on more coding and execution, the center of gravity of engineers' value moves. The Qwen team in Alibaba's handbook summed up three-plus months of change this way: the biggest change was not "writing code faster" but that the focus of development work shifted — engineers must put more energy into problem definition, context organization, boundary design, documentation and result verification.

Core Mechanisms

1. How individual roles change.

  • From writing code to defining problems: turn vague business intent into a complete, precise, verifiable Spec.
  • From implementation to designing verification: decide what counts as correct and how to prove it automatically.
  • From execution to review and decision: review agent output and make judgments at key gates.
  • From a single stack to end-to-end ownership: the handbook notes AI broadens individual capability, and teams gradually move from "many people dividing up one requirement" to "one individual owning a requirement end to end", which cuts some communication cost but raises the bar for systems thinking and accountability for results.

2. Why organizations must change. The handbook revisits the premises behind several classic laws: Conway's law (organizational structure determines system structure because communication within a team is far cheaper than across teams), Brooks's "adding people to a late project makes it later" (communication cost grows rapidly with headcount), and Taylor's specialization (human attention is scarce). Its judgment: AI has no communication decay, needs no incentives, doesn't tire and has tiny context-switching cost, so the premises of every "design built on human constraints" are starting to fail.

3. Organizational directions the handbook proposes (the authors' views, still being explored):

  • Merging specialist roles: no longer strictly separating product, design, frontend and backend; encouraging problem-focused "super individuals".
  • 3–5 person small teams: with a clear owner, charging at a specific problem, possibly organized as projects.
  • A changed role for managers: motivation, coaching, hiring, exits and culture-building still have to be done by people.
  • Divide and conquer: new businesses win on speed, established ones emphasize stability and safety, so the degree of management change should differ.

Key Papers and Milestones

  • Conway's law, The Mythical Man-Month: classic accounts of how organization and software structure relate
  • Alibaba's AI Native R&D Paradigm Practice Handbook: organizational capability (Challenge 5)
  • 2025 METR randomized controlled trial: a reminder that perceived individual productivity can be the opposite of measured productivity

Engineering Practice and Numbers

  • New core skills: requirement clarification and Spec writing, building context assets (rules, Skills, project maps), test and verification design, reviewing agent output, risk judgment.
  • Evaluation must change too: the handbook notes that if you say "judgment is worth more than execution" but KPIs still measure output volume, the transformation won't land.
  • The real cost of transformation: the handbook confronts "distillation anxiety" — every SOP an employee writes, every process they teach AI, exports knowledge into organizational assets; when employees realize "the more I say, the faster I'm replaced", key knowledge gets hidden, yet context-building depends precisely on employees voicing tacit conventions. The handbook also notes entry-level roles face challenges, which in the long run could dry up the pipeline of senior talent.

Common Misconceptions

  • "AI will put engineers out of work, so why learn?" The focus moves to defining problems and designing verification, which depend even more on engineering experience.
  • "Merging roles means layoffs." The handbook stresses being explicit about how AI gains are shared and providing real mechanisms to "catch" people in transition.
  • "Organizational change can be copied." The handbook says plainly there is no standard answer in this area yet.
