Roles and Organisation: From Writing Code to Defining Problems and Designing Verification

Companion to diagram #37 · Chapter 6 "How AI Reshapes the Dev Lifecycle"

As agents take on more coding and execution, engineers' value shifts. The Qwen team in Alibaba's practice handbook summarised three-plus months of change like this: the biggest change was not "writing code faster" but a shift in the centre of gravity of development work. Engineers need to put more effort into defining problems, organising context, designing boundaries, writing clearly and verifying results.

This article works at two levels: how individual roles change, and why organisations must change.

1. Individuals: four shifts

1. From writing code to defining problems. Chapter 4's spec-driven approach showed that the faster agents write, the more a misread requirement costs. Turning vague business intent into a complete, precise, verifiable spec has become one of the most important skills. Can "let documents be shared publicly" be broken down into explicit decisions about revocation, expiry, permissions and idempotency?

2. From implementing to designing verification. Deciding what counts as correct and how to prove it automatically. Chapter 3 concluded that "the more autonomy, the more automated verification", so whoever designs verification effectively decides how far agents can be let off the leash.

3. From executing to reviewing and deciding. Reviewing agent output and making the call at key gates. Review is not reading code line by line; it is judging whether the approach makes business sense, whether the tests are enough to support delivery, and where the risks are.

4. From one stack to end-to-end ownership. The handbook notes that AI widens individual capability: teams move from "several people dividing up one requirement" towards "one person owning a requirement end to end". That removes some communication cost but raises the bar for systems thinking and accountability. A back-end engineer can write front-end code with AI, but must be able to judge whether it is good.

These new skills form a checklist: requirement clarification and spec writing, building context assets (rules, Skills, project maps), test and verification design, reviewing agent output, and risk judgement. They share one trait: they depend more on engineering experience, not less.

2. Organisations: the premises of classic laws are loosening

The handbook revisits several classic laws and the premises behind them:

  • Conway's Law: organisational structure determines system structure. The premise: communication within a team is far cheaper than across teams.
  • Brooks's Law (The Mythical Man-Month): adding people to a late project makes it later. The premise: communication cost grows fast with headcount; n people have n(n−1)/2 communication paths, 10 for 5 people and 45 for 10.
  • Taylor's specialisation: the premise is that human attention is scarce, so specialisation raises efficiency.

The handbook's assessment: AI has no communication decay, needs no incentives, doesn't tire and has tiny context-switching costs. Every design premised on human limitations is seeing its premise weaken. That doesn't make the laws wrong; it means the conditions they rely on change when part of the workforce is agents.

3. Organisational directions proposed by the handbook

These are the handbook authors' views and still exploratory:

  • Merging specialist roles: no strict separation of product, design, front end and back end, encouraging problem-focused "super-individuals".
  • Small teams of 3–5: a clear owner, charging at a specific problem, possibly organised as a project. Back to Brooks: 5 people have only 10 communication paths.
  • A changed role for managers: motivation, coaching, hiring, exits and culture-building must still be done by people.
  • Divide and conquer: new businesses win on speed, established ones stress stability and safety, so the degree of management change should differ.

4. The real costs of transformation

The handbook doesn't dodge the hard parts, and this section is especially worth reading:

Evaluation must change too. If you say "judgement is worth more than execution" but KPIs still measure output volume, the transformation won't stick. People work the way they are measured.

"Distillation anxiety". Every SOP an employee writes and every process they teach AI exports their knowledge into organisational assets. When employees realise "the more I say, the faster I'm replaced", key knowledge gets hidden, yet building context depends on people voicing tacit conventions. It is a real dilemma: Chapter 4 calls context engineering the key to 1→N, but context engineering depends on people being willing to share. The handbook stresses making clear how the gains from AI will be shared and providing a real mechanism to "catch" people through the transition.

Entry-level roles. The handbook notes that entry-level roles face challenges, which in the long run could drain the pipeline of senior talent. Today's senior engineers grew up writing lots of basic code; if AI writes the basic code, where will newcomers gain experience? There is no answer yet.

5. Common misconceptions

  • "AI will put engineers out of work, so why learn?" The weight shifts to defining problems and designing verification, which depend more on engineering experience.
  • "Merging roles means layoffs." The handbook stresses making clear how AI gains are shared and offering a real transition path.
  • "Organisational change can be copied." The handbook says plainly there is no standard answer yet.
  • "If I feel faster, I am faster." METR's 2025 RCT reminds us that subjective impressions of personal productivity can contradict measurement (Chapter 3).
