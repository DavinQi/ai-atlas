Metrics: Measure Delivery Outcomes, Not the Share of AI-Written Code

Companion to diagram #27 · Chapter 4 "AI Coding Today"

When teams push AI into development, the first two numbers they reach for are: how many people use AI, and how much code AI wrote. Alibaba's practice handbook notes that these help during cold start, but a metrics system built only around them goes astray: code written by AI is not necessarily committed, committed code is not necessarily released, and released code does not necessarily deliver value. The real question is not "how much AI was used" but "what did AI change?"

1. How a metric goes wrong

Imagine a team makes "AI code share" a quarterly target, raising it from 30% to 60%. What happens?

  • Engineers discover that having AI generate verbose boilerplate, lots of comments and tests raises the share fastest.
  • Logic that took three lines becomes thirty AI-generated lines.
  • At quarter end the share reaches 65%, beating the target. But the codebase is bigger, reviews are slower, and the defect rate may even have risen.

This is a version of Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The handbook lists the perverse incentive of each single metric: install rate counts users who installed but do no meaningful development; session counts mistake ad-hoc Q&A for productivity; AI code share actively rewards useless generation.

2. A three-layer metrics system

The handbook's answer is three layers, each answering a different question:

  • L3 value delivery: requirement lead time, change lead time, release frequency, AI vs non-AI delivery. Answers: is AI making the business faster and more stable?
  • L2 engineering quality: AI defect rate, rollbacks and rework, self-repaired defects, risk incidents. Answers: did L1 growth come at the expense of quality?
  • L1 AI effectiveness: coverage, sessions, tokens, AI lines, Skills, MCP, context assets. Answers: is the team really using it, and are problems in the tools or the context?

The layers cannot be read in isolation. L1 alone encourages "AI for AI's sake"; L2 alone becomes a quality audit; L3 alone cannot explain where changes came from. L1 shows AI entered the process, L2 shows quality risk is contained, L3 shows business results improved; together they form a complete chain of evidence.

Apply this to the example in section 1: L1 (AI share) rose, L2 (defects, rework) worsened, L3 (lead time) did not improve. Seen together, the problem is obvious.

3. Aligning with DORA

L3 and L2 closely match the widely used DORA software delivery metrics:

  • Throughput: change lead time (from commit to production deployment), deployment frequency, failed deployment recovery time.
  • Instability: change failure rate, deployment rework rate (the share of unplanned deployments caused by production incidents).

The advantage of DORA is that it was widely used long before AI coding, so teams often already have historical data and can compare directly before and after introducing AI.

4. How do you attribute?

If L3 improved, how do you know AI caused it rather than simpler requirements or process changes? The handbook describes the collection method: lightweight hooks adapt to each AI tool and convert activity into standard events, which are then attributed along Session → Commit → Change → Workitem. A released requirement (workitem) can be traced to the changes it comprises, the commits behind those changes, and the AI sessions in which the commits were produced. Only then can "AI-assisted delivery" and "non-AI delivery" be compared fairly.

5. Why surveys are not enough

Chapter 3 introduced METR's 2025 RCT: 16 experienced open-source developers working in mature projects they knew well took 19% longer when AI was allowed, though they had expected AI to cut time by 24% and still estimated afterwards that it had cut 20%. An equally rigorous 2023 Copilot trial found a well-bounded experimental task done 55.8% faster.

These studies teach two lessons for measurement: first, subjective impressions can point in the opposite direction from measurement, so objective data is essential; second, effects depend heavily on context, so do not apply someone else's numbers to yourself; measure in your own workflow.

6. Practical points

  • No individual rankings. Once you rank people, the team optimises the metric instead of real collaboration.
  • Measure context assets too. Are Skills, MCP, specs, READMEs / runbooks, templates and checklists reused, updated and validated? A Skill nobody uses might as well not exist.
  • Get a baseline first. Record current lead time and rework before introducing a new process, or there is nothing to compare against.

7. Common misconceptions

  • "The higher the AI code share, the better." It measures usage, not value, and can reward useless generation.
  • "Shorter lead times prove AI works." They may come from process changes or simpler requirements; attribution needs process evidence.
  • "A survey is enough." Impressions and measurements can disagree.
