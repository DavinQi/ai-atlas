Pretraining and Scaling Laws: Predict the Next Token, Trade Scale for Lower Loss

Companion to diagram #6 · Chapter 1 "How LLMs Are Trained"

Pretraining is the most expensive step in the pipeline. A frontier-scale run occupies tens of thousands of GPUs for months, with almost no room to "try again". So before pressing start, how does a team decide how big the model should be, how much data to feed it and what level to expect? Diagram #6 gives the skeleton of the answer: the objective is next-token cross-entropy; the loss curve is roughly a straight line on log-log axes; Chinchilla gives a ratio of "about 20 tokens per parameter"; and C≈6ND ties parameters, data and compute together. This article explains them in the order an engineering decision is made.

1. Get the arithmetic right: where does C≈6ND come from?

Let the model have N parameters and train on D tokens. When one token passes forward through the model, each parameter takes part in roughly one multiplication and one addition, about 2N floating-point operations; back-propagation computes two sets of gradients (with respect to activations and to parameters), about twice the forward pass, or 4N. Together that is about 6N per token, so total compute is:

C≈6ND

This approximation appears in Kaplan et al.'s scaling-law paper (counting non-embedding parameters only). It's simple enough for mental arithmetic yet very accurate. Plug in GPT-3: 6×1.75×1011×3×1011≈3.15×1023 FLOPs, against 3.14×1023 reported in the paper. Plug in Llama 3 405B: 6×4.05×1011×1.56×1013≈3.8×1025, matching the reported 3.8×1025 FLOPs. With this formula, any "model size × tokens read" plan converts immediately into compute, and from there into GPU-hours and money (article 8 of this chapter).

2. Power laws: why is the loss a straight line?

In 2020 Kaplan et al. systematically measured how cross-entropy relates to scale and found that, over more than seven orders of magnitude, test loss falls smoothly as a power law in parameters N, data D and compute C:

L(N)=(NNc​​)αN​, αN​≈0.076;L(D)=(DDc​​)αD​, αD​≈0.095

A power law is a straight line on log-log axes; that is what the curve in the diagram means. What do such small exponents imply? A quick calculation: 10× more parameters multiplies loss by 10−0.076≈84%; 10× more data by about 80%; 10× more compute, optimally allocated, by only about 89%.

Two direct consequences follow. First, predictability: run a batch of cheap small models, fit the line and extrapolate to predict roughly how low a large model's loss will go, then decide how much to invest. Llama 3 did exactly this, fitting scaling laws on its own data to conclude that 405B was roughly compute-optimal for its budget. Second, noticeably better requires several orders of magnitude more scale. Each 10× buys only a small drop, which is the fundamental reason models keep getting bigger. Kaplan also found that over a wide range, architectural details like width and depth barely affect loss; scale itself is the protagonist.

3. Chinchilla: spend on parameters or on data?

Fix compute C and N and D trade off: a bigger model can read less. Kaplan's conclusion favoured training very large models on relatively little data and stopping well before convergence. In 2022 DeepMind's Chinchilla study redid the experiment: more than 400 models (from 70 million to over 16 billion parameters, 5 to 500 billion tokens), fitting a formula that splits loss into three terms:

L(N,D)=E+N0.34A​+D0.28B​,E=1.69, A=406.4, B=410.7

Each term means something: E is the irreducible part, the entropy of natural text itself, which no model can remove; the second term is the loss from "the model isn't big enough"; the third from "there isn't enough data". Minimising this for fixed compute gives the conclusion: parameters and training tokens should scale in equal proportion; double the model, double the data.

Using the same compute as the 280-billion-parameter Gopher, they trained Chinchilla with only 70 billion parameters but 1.4 trillion tokens. Chinchilla won across a large set of downstream tasks, averaging 67.5% on MMLU, more than 7 points above Gopher. 1.4T ÷ 70B = 20: that is where the rule of thumb "about 20 tokens per parameter" comes from, and it revealed that large models of the time were generally "under-trained".

Why did the two studies differ? The Chinchilla paper attributes part of the gap to Kaplan's experiments using a fixed learning-rate schedule and token count for all models, which overestimated large models trained on little data. The more important engineering lesson: the ratio depends on your data and training setup, so refit on your own data rather than copying a number.

4. Why are today's models often "over-trained"?

If 20 tokens per parameter is optimal, why did Llama 3's 8B model read about 15 trillion tokens, far beyond that ratio?

Because Chinchilla optimised only one thing: training compute. A model is trained once but called countless times, and the cost of each call depends on parameter count. Training a smaller model on more data spends more compute up front in exchange for cheaper, faster requests for the rest of its life. The Chinchilla paper itself notes that smaller models make fine-tuning and inference cheaper. So "optimal" depends on what you optimise: if the model will serve huge volumes of requests, over-training a small model is often the better deal.

5. Will loss fall to zero?

No, and the reason goes back to information theory. Cross-entropy measures how many bits it takes to encode real text using the model's estimates, and it can never fall below the entropy of the text itself: what an author will write next, what someone's name is, tomorrow's news, all involve genuine randomness. Chinchilla's fitted E=1.69 (in nats per token, about 2.4 bits) is an estimate of exactly that part. Scaling laws describe how a model's cross-entropy approaches the entropy of language itself, the number Shannon set out to measure. (This number is per token and depends on the dataset and tokenization, so it can't be compared directly with Shannon's per-letter estimates.)

6. How do hundreds of billions of parameters fit on a cluster?

When a model is too large for one GPU, training becomes a distributed-systems problem. Megatron-LM combines three kinds of parallelism: data parallelism (each group of GPUs processes different batches), tensor parallelism (split one layer's matrices across several GPUs) and pipeline parallelism (place different layers on different GPUs and pass activations along like an assembly line). ZeRO goes further, partitioning optimiser states, gradients and parameters across data-parallel processes to remove redundant copies and save a great deal of memory. These techniques decide whether tens of thousands of GPUs are used effectively, and they directly affect the final bill.

7. Three things easily misread

"Emergent abilities are sudden jumps." Wei et al. documented abilities absent in small models that appeared suddenly in large ones. But Schaeffer et al. pointed out that many "jumps" come from discontinuous metrics (such as counting only fully correct answers); with continuous metrics the curves become smooth and predictable. Both views have evidence; the key is to see which metric is being used.

"Scaling laws guarantee downstream ability." The power laws describe cross-entropy loss. How downstream task scores relate to loss must be evaluated separately, and tasks differ greatly in sensitivity.

"Bigger is better." Scaling laws hold "all else being equal". Change data quality, mixture or training setup and the curve moves. FineWeb-Edu shows that better data effectively shifts the whole line down.
