The Future Shape: Humans Set Goals and Acceptance, Agent Teams Deliver

Companion to diagram #36 · Chapter 6 "How AI Reshapes the Dev Lifecycle"

A note first: this article is a trend assessment, not established fact. The diagram's future shape has people defining goals and acceptance criteria while agent teams do most of the delivery from requirement clarification to release and archiving, with people stepping in only at key points. The view extends the practices in Chapters 4 and 5 and matches the evolution path described in Alibaba's practice handbook, which itself admits that many of these directions are "visible as trends but not yet working end to end".

So the focus here is not to paint a pretty picture but to answer: what is still missing to get there?

1. Three stages

Per Alibaba's handbook, human–agent collaboration evolves in roughly three stages:

  1. Full assistance: people use coding agents to do the work, and people stay in charge. This is where most teams are today.
  2. Cloud autonomy: cloud-hosted agents receive a task, then independently pull code, run environments, produce changes and wait for review. Several products already work at this stage, such as the Copilot cloud coding agent mentioned in Chapter 3.
  3. Multi-agent collaboration: several agents in different roles handle clarification, design, coding, testing, release and archiving, with people stepping in only at key points.

The handbook notes that this path is essentially a continual outward shift of the trust boundary: from the person, to the cloud runtime, to agent teams, toolchains, task state and cross-system authorisation. Each step outward demands stronger isolation, authorisation and observability to back that trust.

2. Two paths: embed in the old process, or reorganise

The handbook distinguishes two paths:

  • Workflow retrofitting (the current mainstream): embed AI into the existing development process. Each node gets a local speed-up, but the whole still runs along a process designed for people.
  • The agentic direction (seen as more promising): agents obtain verification signals themselves and keep advancing the task on that basis. Process still exists, but it mainly handles scheduling, permissions, state, audit and high-risk checks, rather than prescribing how the agent thinks at each step.

The handbook draws an analogy with electrification: early factories simply replaced the steam engine with an electric motor, keeping the line shafts and layout; the real productivity leap came after every machine got its own motor and production was reorganised.

The analogy fits well. In a steam-age factory every machine connected through a long line shaft to one central engine, so the layout had to follow the shaft. Swap in one central electric motor and the shaft and layout stay; the gains are limited. Only when each machine had its own small motor could the shaft be removed and the factory laid out by the logic of the work.

For software: many of today's processes (requirement review meetings, staged hand-offs to QA, hand-offs organised by job title) were designed for "limited manpower and expensive communication". Inserting AI into each step is like swapping in an electric motor; the real change may require redesigning the process itself.

3. Prerequisites: what is still missing

For agent teams to deliver reliably, at least four things are needed, each corresponding to an earlier chapter:

Prerequisite Meaning Related chapter
Reproducible, callable development environments Agents can build, start services, prepare data, drive browsers, read logs and metrics on their own Ch. 4 layered verification, Ch. 5 worktrees
A layered verification system Second- and minute-level machine feedback; people look only at what machines can't judge Ch. 4
Agent identity, delegated authorisation and end-to-end observability Knowing which agent did what on whose behalf Ch. 5 digital employees
Structured organisational knowledge Knowledge agents can retrieve, understand and cite Ch. 4 context engineering

4. Real-world constraints

Technically. Anthropic notes that most coding tasks have limited parallelisable parts and that LLM agents are not yet good at real-time coordination and delegation.

In adoption. The handbook's teams went through a curve from excitement to pragmatism, hitting four sticking points: environments that won't come up, context that won't come together, verification that won't run, and releases stuck in process. Notably, none of the four is "the model isn't smart enough".

The handbook also sees three gaps between model capability and gains in enterprise R&D efficiency: agent infrastructure, context and organisation. Model progress fills only a small part of them.

5. Preparation you can do today

The future shape hasn't arrived, but preparing for it pays off now, and these steps improve efficiency even if it never arrives:

  • Turn internal systems into tools agents can call (MCP / CLI / Skills).
  • Make verification give feedback in seconds and minutes.
  • Give agents their own identities and least privilege.

The handbook advises accepting the uncertainty of fast technical change while not postponing necessary infrastructure while "waiting for a better model".

6. Common misconceptions

  • "The future is fully unmanned." The handbook's team says plainly that AI Native is not about removing people but about redrawing the responsibilities of systems, AI and people.
  • "A strong enough model will make it happen." Of the three gaps, model capability is only a small part.
  • "This is far off." The second stage (cloud autonomy) is already in use across several products.
